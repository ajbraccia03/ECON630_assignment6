Defining an inversion episode¶

The spread crosses zero more often than there are distinct inversions — the 2006–07 episode dips below zero, pops barely positive for a month or two, and dips again. Treating each crossing as its own episode would invent inversions that nobody would describe as separate.

Judgment call, not in Plan.md: runs of spread < 0 separated by a gap of three months or less are merged into one episode, and single-month dips are kept rather than filtered out. That second choice matters — both 1990-03 and 1998-06 are one-month dips, and dropping them would discard one real signal and the clearest false alarm in the sample. MAX_GAP is set as a named constant so the sensitivity of the results to this choice can be checked.

Lead time is measured from the first month of an episode to the first month of the next recession.

In [7]:
MAX_GAP = 3   # months of non-inversion tolerated inside a single episode

inverted = spread < 0
run_id   = (inverted != inverted.shift()).cumsum()
runs     = [(g.index[0], g.index[-1]) for _, g in inverted[inverted].groupby(run_id[inverted])]

episodes = []
for start, end in runs:
    if episodes:
        prev_end = episodes[-1][1]
        gap = (start.year - prev_end.year) * 12 + (start.month - prev_end.month) - 1
        if gap <= MAX_GAP:
            episodes[-1] = (episodes[-1][0], end)
            continue
    episodes.append((start, end))

# recession onsets: the month USREC flips 0 -> 1
onsets = list(usrec.index[(usrec == 1) & (usrec.shift(1) == 0)])


def months_between(a, b):
    return (b.year - a.year) * 12 + (b.month - a.month)


LAST = spread.index[-1]
rows = []
for start, end in episodes:
    following = [d for d in onsets if d > start]
    rows.append({
        "start": start,
        "end":   end,
        "recession": following[0] if following else pd.NaT,
        "lead":  months_between(start, following[0] if following else LAST),
        "min_spread": spread.loc[start:end].min(),
    })
ep = pd.DataFrame(rows)

assert len(ep) > 0, "no inversion episodes found - check the sign of spread"
assert (ep["lead"] > 0).all(), "a recession appears to precede its own inversion onset"

Test 1 — Two-sample t-test¶

Compare the spread twelve months earlier in months that turned out to be recession months against months that did not. Welch's version (equal_var=False) is used because the two groups differ enormously in size and there is no reason to assume equal variances.

The sign is what the yield-curve story predicts: the spread a year before a recession month averages 0.34 percentage points, against 1.07 in non-recession months — about three-quarters of a point flatter.

But that p-value is not worth much, and it is worth being precise about why. The t-test treats all 428 months as independent draws. They are nothing of the sort: the 31 recession months are four contiguous episodes, and both the spread and recession status are strongly autocorrelated from one month to the next. The effective sample size is closer to four than to 428, so the test overstates its confidence by a wide margin. The difference in means is a fair description; the p-value beside it is not.

Test 2 — Logistic regression¶

USREC ~ spread_lag12, one lag only. This goes past what has been lectured — statsmodels appeared in the toolkit overview but was not taught — so each piece is spelled out, and it is flagged as self-directed in the writeup.

  • sm.add_constant adds the intercept column. Without it the model is forced through zero, which silently changes what the coefficient means.
  • Logit(y, X).fit() estimates by maximum likelihood.
  • The coefficient is on the log-odds scale, so np.exp(coef) converts it to an odds ratio per one-percentage-point change in the spread.

Test 2b — The same model with autocorrelation-robust standard errors¶

Not in Plan.md, added because the plan's own stated limitation demands it. The table above reports Covariance Type: nonrobust, which assumes independent observations — the same assumption the t-test broke. Re-fitting with Newey-West (HAC) standard errors at a 12-month bandwidth keeps the coefficient identical and asks only how much of its apparent precision survives.

In [14]:

    "std error":   [logit.bse["spread_lag12"],    logit_hac.bse["spread_lag12"]],
    "z":           [logit.tvalues["spread_lag12"], logit_hac.tvalues["spread_lag12"]],

The coefficient does not move — the point estimate is what it is. The standard error more than doubles, and the p-value goes from 0.0001 to 0.09. Under the conventional 5% threshold the relationship stops being significant.

This is the single most important number in the notebook, and it is the one a naive reading would have missed entirely. The nonrobust table is not wrong arithmetic; it is answering a question nobody should ask of four recessions.

Conclusion¶

The question: does a negative 10-year minus 2-year spread reliably precede U.S. recessions, or is it noisier than its reputation suggests?

On this evidence, noisier — which is what Plan.md expected going in.

The direction of the relationship is real and consistent. The spread a year before a recession month is about three-quarters of a percentage point flatter than otherwise, the logit coefficient is negative as theory predicts, and four of the five inversion episodes since 1990 were followed by a recession. Nothing here says the signal is meaningless.

What it is not is reliable, in the sense of supporting a forecast:

  1. Lead times do not cluster. 5, 34, 14, and 23 months. Knowing an inversion has begun tells you remarkably little about when.
  2. It produces false alarms. The one-month dip in June 1998 was followed by 34 quiet months, and by the time the 2001 recession arrived a separate, deeper inversion had been running for over a year.
  3. It misses recessions. The curve was positive throughout the two years before the 2020 recession. A signal that cannot see a pandemic coming is a signal with a bounded domain.
  4. Its most recent firing has not resolved. The 2022–24 inversion is the deepest in the sample at −0.93 and, 49 months on, has not been followed by a recession.
  5. The statistical significance does not survive an honest standard error. p = 0.0001 becomes p = 0.09 once autocorrelation is allowed for.

The limitation Plan.md flagged up front turned out to be the finding. With four recessions in thirty-six years, there was never enough independent variation to support a confident causal claim, and the gap between the naive and HAC standard errors is that shortage made visible. The charts and the difference in means describe the pattern well. The p-values should not be read as confirming it.

What would have changed the conclusion: lead times falling in a narrow band, no unexplained episodes, and a coefficient that stayed significant under robust standard errors. None of the three holds.

How I used Claude Code¶

The workflow¶

I wrote Plan.md before any code existed — question, data sources, cleaning steps, validation checks, charts, tests, and what would count as evidence for or against my expectation that the signal would prove noisy. I also wrote NOTES.md, a template for logging each stage as I reviewed it, with a stage-by-stage list of what to watch for.

Then I worked in four stages — data pull and validation, cleaning, charts, tests — and after each one I stopped, reviewed the code, and wrote the log entry before starting the next. The logs in NOTES.md were written at the time, not reconstructed at the end.

The plan-first part mattered more than I expected. Because the specification already existed, I could ask for one stage at a time and check the output against something I had written while thinking about the economics rather than the code. It also meant disagreements were concrete: not "this looks off" but "the plan says GS10 - GS2 and this is the other way around."

Three things that actually happened¶

1. My own plan contained a factual error, and the data caught it.

Plan.md specified a spot check asserting the 10y–2y spread was negative in September 2007, ahead of the Great Recession. It is not — that month is +0.51. The inversion ran from 2006-02 to 2007-05, and by September 2007 the Fed had started cutting and the curve had re-steepened. I had written the assert from memory of the narrative (inversion, then recession) without checking the month.

This is the case for spot-check asserts working in both directions. I wrote the check to catch bad data; it caught a bad belief of mine instead.

2. I had to push back on the fix, and I was right to.

Claude Code's replacement was (spread.loc["2006"] < 0).any() — "inverted somewhere in 2006." Its reasoning was that the 2006 inversion is shallow (deepest −0.14) and pinning the test to a single month is fragile. I didn't accept that. The plan's whole approach was specific named months, and the fragility argument doesn't hold: Treasury constant-maturity rates aren't revised, so 2006-11 is as stable a reference as any, and −0.14 is unambiguously negative. We used the specific month.

The pattern worth noting: it made a correct diagnosis and then a slightly worse repair than the situation called for. Catching that required knowing what the plan was for, not just whether the code ran.

3. A chart followed the plan and was worse for it.

Plan.md asked for lead times shown as ax.annotate arrows on the spread line. Built as specified, the arrows floated above the series, disconnected from the episodes they described, and the "2020 recession, no prior inversion" callout pointed at blank space — because that recession is two months long and invisible on a 36-year axis. Claude Code rendered it, said it didn't work, and rebuilt it as a per-episode timeline.

I accepted the redesign. The plan's goal for that chart was "the lead time is visible at a glance for each episode," and the plan's method didn't achieve it. Worth recording that the departure was from the method, not the goal.

The thing I'd have missed¶

Plan.md flagged a limitation up front: four recessions is not much to work with, so the regression should be read as description rather than a powered test. I wrote that as a hedge to put in the conclusion.

Claude Code took it as an instruction. Both tests in the plan assume independent observations, and monthly recession data plainly isn't — the 31 recession months are four contiguous episodes. It re-ran the logit with Newey-West standard errors:

coefficient std error p-value
nonrobust −1.049 0.265 0.0001
HAC (12 lags) −1.049 0.623 0.092

The point estimate doesn't move. The standard error more than doubles and the relationship stops being significant at 5%.

That is the most important number in the notebook, and I would not have produced it. Left alone I'd have reported p = 0.0001, mentioned the small-sample caveat in a sentence, and moved on — a result that looks far stronger than the evidence supports. The limitation I wrote as a disclaimer turned out to be the finding.

It's worth being clear about why I trust this one: it cuts against the more impressive result. A tool that only ever strengthens your conclusions is one to be suspicious of.

Checking for the failure mode the Korinek reading warns about¶

The specific risk is plausible-looking fabrication — numbers that read correctly but were never computed. I checked for it deliberately at each stage:

  • Every figure traces to a live fred.get_series call. Nothing is hardcoded, no CSVs are cached in the repo, and the notebook re-pulls on every run.
  • Series IDs are GS10, GS2, USREC — checked against FRED, not assumed.
  • No .fillna() or .dropna() appears anywhere on the data path except the single .dropna() that removes the twelve rows the lag necessarily leaves empty.
  • The API key is read from a gitignored .env via os.getenv and never appears in the notebook.

It didn't fabricate anything. But the check that mattered most wasn't any of those — it was the 2007-09 assert failing, which is the one moment where a wrong number would have been caught by the code rather than by me reading it.

Where it was weakest¶

Layout, consistently. The first version of every chart had its title overlapping its subtitle. It caught these by rendering the images and looking at them, which worked — but it's a reminder that code running without error and code producing something usable are different standards.

It also broke twice on pandas edge cases that a person would likely have avoided: None in a datetime column becoming NaT, so x is None silently returns False; and .loc["2001-01"] on a monthly series returning a one-element Series rather than a scalar, so comparing two of them raises rather than returning a boolean. Both surfaced as loud errors rather than wrong answers, which is the good kind of failure.

What I'd do differently¶

Write the validation asserts against the data rather than from memory. My 2007-09 error came from writing a spot check about a period I hadn't looked at. Thirty seconds with the series first would have caught it, and the assert would then have been doing its real job — guarding against a bad pull — instead of encoding a mistake.

I'd also define terms in the plan more carefully. Plan.md asked for lead times "for each episode" without ever saying what separates one episode from the next, and the data doesn't cooperate: the 2006–07 inversion dips below zero, pops positive for a month, and dips again. That definition (MAX_GAP = 3, keeping single-month dips) had to be invented mid-analysis, and every lead time in the notebook depends on it. Deciding it in advance, with no results visible, would have been a more honest place to make that call.
